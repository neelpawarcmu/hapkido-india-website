The art

Introduction to Hapkido

Hapkido is a dynamic and comprehensive Korean martial art, often described as “the art of coordinated power.” It blends striking, joint locks, throws, and pressure-point techniques into a fluid, practical method of self-defence.

Unlike arts built for a single range, Hapkido is meant to work whether an opponent is at kicking distance, within punching range, or has grabbed you in a close-quarters clinch. It is more than a set of fighting techniques. It is a disciplined way of harmonising mind, body, and spirit, so that conflict is met with wisdom and efficiency.

The name

Hapkido comes from three Korean words. Together they are the art’s foundation: meet force by harmonising with it, apply energy with intent, and walk a path that includes moral growth.

합

Hap — Harmony

Coordinate with an opponent’s force instead of meeting it head-on. Redirect their energy and use it against them.

기

Ki — Energy

Cultivate internal power, breath, and focused intention so each technique carries more than muscle alone.

도

Do — The Way

A path of personal and ethical development. Physical skill and moral growth are practised together.

A complete fighting system

A Hapkido practitioner’s toolkit is deliberately wide. These six areas are trained as one system, not as separate arts.

Joint locks

The hallmark of the art. Wrist, elbow, shoulder, knee, and ankle techniques control, subdue, or disable an attacker with minimal force.

Throws and takedowns

Throws built on the opponent’s momentum and balance, often continuing directly from a joint lock.

Striking

Precise strikes with the hands, feet, knees, and elbows, often aimed at vital points and nerve centres.

Kicking

A broad kicking repertoire: low, practical kicks to the legs and knees, and high, spinning, and jumping kicks.

Pressure points

Knowledge of vital points used to amplify pain, create an opening, or disrupt an attacker’s ability to continue.

Weapons

Advanced practice with the short stick (dan bong), cane, staff, and rope — Hapkido principles applied to objects at hand.

Training and mindset

Adaptability

Flow from one technique to the next according to what the opponent actually does.

Practicality

Techniques are built for real self-defence, including against a larger and stronger attacker.

Control

Choose the level of response, from a gentle restraint to a decisive disabling technique.

Who Hapkido is for

Hapkido suits people of all ages, sizes, and physical abilities. Because the art uses the attacker’s force, strength and size matter less than technique, timing, and leverage. It appeals to anyone who wants a realistic self-defence system, a rigorous physical training, and a traditional martial art with deep philosophical roots.

The art

The History of Hapkido

Hapkido is a Korean martial art of the twentieth century, born from cultural exchange, synthesis, and modern evolution. Its roots run through both Korean and Japanese martial traditions.

  1. Choi Yong-Sool, the founder

    The creation of Hapkido is credited to Choi Yong-Sool (최용술, 1904–1986). Taken to Japan as a boy from Korean-occupied Manchuria, he spent more than thirty years there. He said he was adopted by Takeda Sokaku, master of Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu, and served as Takeda’s attendant and student. The joint manipulation and throws he learned became Hapkido’s technical core.

  2. Post-war Korea, and a new art

    After the Second World War, Choi returned to Korea and began teaching what he first called Yawara, or Yoo Sool — Korean for jujutsu. His joint locks and throws drew early students. Among the most important was Ji Han-Jae, already skilled in Korean traditional kicking (taekkyeon) and the staff. Training under Choi from the late 1940s, Ji added indigenous Korean kicks and strikes and rounded the system out.

  3. 1959 — the name Hapkido

    In 1959, Ji Han-Jae, by then teaching independently, officially named the art Hapkido and established the first formal organisation, the Korea Hapkido Association.

  4. A synthesis, and a dispute

    The art’s character comes from two sources: Choi Yong-Sool’s Daitō-ryū foundation of joint locks, throws, and pressure points, and Ji Han-Jae’s dynamic, circular Korean kicking and weapons practice. The relationship between the two men later became disputed, and both lineages claimed primary credit. Different organisations and stylistic variations followed.

  5. 1965 — a national federation

    On 2 May 1965, leaders of the major Hapkido schools, spearheaded by Ji Han-Jae, formed the Daehan Hapkido Hyub Hwe (Korea Hapkido Association). That date is the founding of what became the Korea Hapkido Federation. Its first chairman was Suh Bok-sub, an early student of Choi Yong-Sool. The federation standardised a core curriculum and Dan ranking, sought government recognition, and placed Hapkido in Korea’s martial heritage alongside Taekwondo.

  6. Into the world

    In the 1960s and 1970s Hapkido was adopted for Korean military and police special forces. Its effectiveness, and its presence in Korean and American action films, carried it abroad. Grandmaster Bong Soo Han brought the art to the United States and choreographed fights for the Billy Jack films. Other masters opened schools worldwide.

  7. Lines that share one root

    Hapkido today is practised in several family lines. Hapkido (합기도) is the mainstream comprehensive art. Hankido (한기도), created by Myung Jae-nam, emphasises fluid, circular, aesthetic motion. Hapkiyusul (합기유술) stays close to the Daitō-ryū material taught by Choi Yong-Sool. The lines differ in emphasis. They share harmony, circular motion, and non-resistance.

The art

Hwa, Won, and Yu

Technique is built on three principles: Hwa (harmony), Won (circle), and Yu (water). Blend with an opponent’s force instead of colliding with it. A smaller person can then defend against a larger, stronger attacker through leverage and redirection. The aim is practical self-defence, not sport fighting.

和

Hwa — Harmony

Non-resistance. Stay relaxed and move with the attack, joining the attacker’s momentum. Direct collision is avoided, energy is saved, and a counter appears.

圓

Won — Circle

When an attack comes in a straight line, a circular, spiral, or helical motion redirects it and adds the attacker’s power to your own. That circle creates leverage for locks, throws, and sweeps.

流

Yu — Water

Adapt, as water does: soft, and powerful when gathered. Flow around the opponent, match their rhythm, and eventually envelop them, as water moves around a stone.

How the principles become technique

Joint locks — Kwan Jeol Ki

Locks are a signature of Hapkido self-defence. Pressure is applied to the wrist, elbow, shoulder, or neck so leverage, not strength, does the work.

When someone grabs or attacks the arm, do not yank away. Blend with the force and flow around it (Hwa and Yu). The first compliance surprises the attacker and takes their balance. Control then comes from a spiral (Won): pressure at an angle the joint already wants to travel, rather than a contest to bend a stiff arm.

Goosul, the wristlock. Meet the grabbing hand, move in the direction of the grab so harmony is kept, then use a tight inward circle to place painful pressure on the wrist and elbow.

Throws — Don Ji Gi

A Hapkido throw sends the attacker off balance and to the ground by using the momentum they brought with them.

The throw begins by stepping with their force (Hwa). If they rush in to push, step into that momentum instead of retreating, so you stay balanced and their forward inertia becomes the throw. You are the pivot of a circle (Won). Part-way through that circle their centre of gravity is lost, and gravity finishes the technique.

Ip Do, the entrance throw. As the attacker lunges, enter their space, pivot in a circle around the hip, and let their forward momentum carry them up and over, with a slight lift, onto their back.

The same three principles run through strikes and kicks as well as locks and throws. Effectiveness does not depend on being the bigger person.

The federation

The Organisations

Hapkido Federation – India stands in a direct line: the Korea Hapkido Federation in Seoul, the International Hapkido Moo Hak Kwan of Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee, and the national body Master Sardar Shaikh built in India.

Korea Hapkido Federation

대한합기도회 · Daehan Hapkido Hwe

The oldest, largest, and most influential governing body for Hapkido. Headquartered in Seoul, it is the national governing body in South Korea, recognised by state institutions and affiliated with the Korean Sports Council and the Korean Olympic Committee.

On 2 May 1965 the leaders of the major schools formed the Daehan Hapkido Hyub Hwe. The first chairman was Suh Bok-sub. The federation created a common core curriculum, a unified Dan system, instructor and Dan certification, and a public face for Hapkido at home and abroad.

It governs sporting competition — sparring, demonstration, and technique — and works to preserve the historical techniques and philosophy of traditional Hapkido. Other federations, including the International Hapkido Federation and the World Hapkido Federation, share the global stage. Practitioners align through their teacher’s lineage. The KHF’s task is to keep combat value and competitive safety in the same art, and to hold technical consistency as Hapkido crosses cultures.

International Hapkido
Moo Hak Kwan

무학관 · School of Martial Study

Moo Hak Kwan is the lineage of Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee: a school of thought as much as a school of technique. The name is deliberate. Moo (武) is martial, Hak (學) is study, Kwan (館) is the hall. Training is a lifelong study, not only a method of fighting.

Grandmaster Lee founded the school in Singapore in 1973. Its international headquarters is in Sydney. He remains lifetime president and chief instructor. Certification of masters and instructors is held centrally, so the curriculum stays intact from school to school.

Its hallmark is Moo Ki Do, a structured weapons curriculum treated as Hapkido itself: the long staff (jang bong), short stick (dan bong), middle staff (jung bong), cane (ji pang e), and Korean sword (hyeop do). Training is grounded in Yu–Won–Hwa, water, circle, and harmony, and in the three spiritual essentials, Shin–Moo–Chong: spirit, martial, and essence. The kwan is affiliated with the European Hapkido Alliance and, through that line, with the Korea Hapkido Federation.

Hapkido Federation – India

The national body

HFI is the nationally recognised governing body for Hapkido in India, founded by Master Sardar Shaikh, the pioneer who first officially introduced the art here. Techniques and certificates come through a direct line: the International Hapkido Moo Hak Kwan under Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee, and through it the Korea Hapkido Federation in Seoul.

Master Shaikh, a 7th Dan in both Hapkido and Taekwondo, has visited KHF headquarters in South Korea many times so that Indian standards stay true to the art’s roots and to its current development.

The federation gives technical guidance, administration, and official certification to state associations and members. It is the channel through which instructors in India receive correct training and globally recognised rank. From beginner to master, the path is structured: personal goals, confidence, and disciplined training.

Instructors are the heart of HFI. Their preparation stresses professional technique and a supportive, approachable way of teaching, so members can train for fitness, self-defence, or mastery and still enjoy the class. HFI has run national championships and instructor and referee seminars, and has hosted Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee in India.

About the Masters

Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee

9th Dan, Kukkiwon · 9th Dan, Korea Hapkido Federation

A 9th Dan is rare. Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee is the first person to hold the 9th Dan from both the Kukkiwon (World Taekwondo Headquarters) and the Korea Hapkido Federation. He has spent more than seventy years building a global legacy for both arts, and he is the founder of the International Hapkido Moo Hak Kwan.

Born in Seoul in 1936, his early training included Judo and Ju-Jitsu under his grandfather. In 1953 he joined Taekwondo Jidokwan, one of the original kwans of modern Taekwondo. He earned his 1st Dan in 1956 and taught Taekwondo for the U.S. 8th Army in Korea. His Hapkido study began in 1961, during service in the Korean army. Under the Korea Hapkido Association he earned his 1st Dan in 1965.

In 1972 the Korea Taekwondo Association selected him as national team coach for Singapore. He led that team at the inaugural World Taekwondo Championships in Seoul in 1973, and in Singapore that year he founded Moo Hak Kwan. In 1974 he emigrated to Australia and established the Australian branches of Taekwondo Jidokwan and Hapkido Moohakkwan. He served as President of the NSW State Taekwondo Association (1975), Vice President of the Australian Taekwondo Association (1978), and President of the Australian Hapkido Federation from 1986. Korea appointed him Head Master of Taekwondo Jidokwan for Australia and recognised him as the foremost authority of the Korea Hapkido Federation there.

Taekwondo

  • 1956 — 1st Dan, Taekwondo Jidokwan
  • 1976 — 7th Dan, World Taekwondo Federation
  • 1993 — 9th Dan, Kukkiwon

Hapkido

  • 1965 — 1st Dan, Korea Hapkido Association
  • 1988 — 8th Dan, Korea Hapkido Federation
  • 1996 — 9th Dan, Korea Hapkido Federation

Further honours include a Bachelor’s degree in Physical Education from Seoul University (1963) and appointment to the Kukkiwon International Advisory Committee (2008).

“Without beauty there is no art. Hapkido is an art so it has to be beautiful.”

His seminars, including the 1999 training in Mumbai, are remembered for energy, knowledge, and humility. He asks practitioners to be role models, to use their skill only for self-defence, and to prefer avoiding conflict over seeking it. Moo Hak Kwan, with its international headquarters in Sydney, continues under his teaching. He still teaches, presides over gradings, and instructs instructors.

Master Sardar Shaikh

7th Dan, Taekwondo · 7th Dan, Hapkido · KHF Director for India

Master Sardar Shaikh’s path began in 1974 and became a mission to establish authentic Korean martial arts in India: from a university athlete to the national master who connects Korea’s tradition with Indian students.

A foundation in two arts

In 1974 he began Judo and, at the same time, Taekwondo under a Korean master. The parallel training, grappling and striking, shaped everything after it. He medalled at the university Judo championship for three consecutive years. He later added Hapkido, drawn to its joint locks, throws, and pressure-point work.

To the source

The search for authentic technique took him first to Nepal and then to South Korea, where he trained under Grandmaster Sung Soo Lee. That apprenticeship gave him advanced technique and the philosophy, history, and principles behind it. The student became the representative. For years he has been the official conduit in India for the International Taekwondo Jidokwan and the International Hapkido Moohakkwan. Repeated visits to South Korea keep the teaching in India current with technical and pedagogical developments at the source.

Responsibility in India

The Korea Hapkido Federation has formally honoured him with two titles: Master, and KHF Director for India. They carry the duty of overseeing standards, promotion, and the growth of authentic Hapkido across the country. He holds 7th Dan in both Taekwondo and Hapkido, and he is preparing for his 8th Dan test. He founded Hapkido Federation – India, and he was the first to officially introduce Hapkido to the country.

A living link

Korea Hapkido Federation · Moo Hak Kwan · India

Grandmaster Lee is a living bridge from the original kwans of Korea to the modern, global community. Master Shaikh is that bridge in India. Their association is the reason HFI can certify students to an international standard and can host the source of the art, not only describe it.
